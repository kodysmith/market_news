# Chapter 7: Entry Mechanics — Getting In Right

## 7.1 The Three Entry Types

Every day trade entry falls into one of three categories. Each has different characteristics, win rates, and risk profiles.

**Breakout:** Buying as the stock breaks above a defined level (ORB high, flat top resistance, flag breakout, prior day high). You're paying up for confirmation.
- Win rate: 40-55% (many false breakouts)
- Average win: Large (catching the start of a move)
- Average loss: Small (stop is tight, just below the breakout level)
- Best when: High volume, strong catalyst, clean pattern
- Risk: False breakouts in choppy markets; entering at the worst price if the breakout is real

**Pullback:** Buying after the stock has pulled back to a defined support level within an established uptrend (VWAP bounce, flag low, 9 EMA touch). You're getting a discount.
- Win rate: 50-60% (better than breakouts because you have a clearer stop)
- Average win: Moderate (you miss the initial move)
- Average loss: Small (stop is below the support level)
- Best when: Clear uptrend established, pullback on declining volume, support is obvious
- Risk: The pullback continues and becomes a reversal; you're catching a falling knife

**Reversal:** Buying at the bottom of a selloff, betting that the selling is exhausted and the stock will bounce. The contrarian play.
- Win rate: 30-40% (most attempted reversals fail)
- Average win: Very large (if you catch the actual bottom)
- Average loss: Can be large if you don't have a clear stop
- Best when: Extreme selling (3+ red candles, 2x+ volume), at major support level, clear catalyst for recovery
- Risk: "Catching a falling knife" — the stock keeps falling. Only for experienced traders with strict stops

**For beginners:** Focus on pullback entries. They have the best win rate and the clearest stop placement. Breakouts are the next progression. Reversals should only be traded after 500+ pullback/breakout trades.

## 7.2 Entry on the Break vs Entry on the Retest

When a stock breaks above a level ($10.50), you have two choices:

**Enter on the break ($10.52):**
- Pro: You don't miss the move if it goes straight up
- Con: If it's a false breakout, you bought the high
- Con: The spread is widest at breakout moments (everyone wants in)
- Best for: High-conviction breakouts with massive volume, where waiting means missing the move

**Enter on the retest ($10.50-10.52 after pulling back from $10.70):**
- Pro: Better price, tighter stop, higher R-multiple
- Con: The stock may not pull back — some breakouts go straight without retesting
- Pro: The retest confirms the breakout — old resistance became new support
- Best for: Patient traders willing to miss some trades for better risk/reward

**The practical approach:** Enter half on the break, half on the retest. This ensures you have a position if it runs, and you can add at a better price if it pulls back. If it pulls back and fails, your loss is smaller because you only had half position.

## 7.3 Time-Based Entries

The time of day significantly affects entry quality. Different time windows have different characteristics:

**9:30-9:35 AM (the open):** Chaos. Spreads are wide, volume is explosive, algorithms are firing. Unless you have a specific open-drive strategy, avoid entering in the first 5 minutes. Let the initial volatility settle.

**9:35-9:45 AM (first pullback):** The initial open-drive often pulls back at 9:35-9:40. This is the first pullback entry for momentum stocks. If a stock gapped up 8%, ran to +10% at 9:32, and pulls back to +7% at 9:38, the 9:38 entry is the first pullback.

**9:45-10:00 AM (opening range formation):** The 15-minute ORB resolves at 9:45. Breakouts of the 15-minute range in this window are the bread and butter of ORB strategies.

**10:00-10:30 AM (second wave):** If the first move stalled and consolidated into a flag/flat top, the second wave breakout happens here. These are often the cleanest entries of the day because the pattern has had time to form.

**10:30-11:00 AM (decision time):** If the stock hasn't made a clear move by 10:30, the probability of a significant intraday move drops sharply. This is where you decide: is this a trend day (keep trading) or a chop day (close positions and stop)?

**11:00 AM-2:00 PM (dead zone):** Avoid new entries. Volume is lowest. Patterns are unreliable. The best traders are not trading during this window — they're reviewing the morning session and planning for the afternoon.

**2:00-3:00 PM (afternoon setups):** Volume picks up. For experienced traders: power-hour setups, late-day breakouts, sector rotation. For newer traders: stay out.

## 7.4 Volume Confirmation

Volume is the single most important confirmation tool for any entry. Here are the rules:

**Breakout volume rule:** The breakout candle must have at least 1.5x the volume of the average candle in the pattern. If the consolidation candles averaged 50K shares, the breakout candle should be at least 75K shares. Ideally 2-3x.

**Increasing volume into the move:** As the stock approaches the breakout level, volume should be building, not declining. Each candle should have more volume than the prior candle. This shows accumulation.

**Declining volume on pullbacks:** During a flag or consolidation, volume should decline. This means sellers are exhausted, not pressing. If volume is increasing on the pullback, sellers are active — the pattern may fail.

**The "volume dry-up" signal:** Immediately before many major breakouts, volume drops to near-zero for 1-3 candles. This "calm before the storm" is one of the most reliable precursors to a strong move. The dry-up occurs because both buyers and sellers are waiting for the breakout — no one wants to act first.

**What low-volume breakouts mean:** A breakout that occurs on declining or below-average volume is suspect. It may be a single large order that temporarily pushed through the level, with no follow-through behind it. These are the breakouts that reverse immediately. Wait for volume to confirm before entering.

## 7.5 The Trigger Candle

A trigger candle provides a specific, objective entry price with a built-in stop level.

**For long entries:**
- Identify a bullish candle at or near a key level (VWAP, support, flag low)
- The trigger price is the HIGH of that candle
- Enter when the next candle breaks above the trigger candle's high
- Stop is the LOW of the trigger candle
- Risk per share = trigger candle high - trigger candle low

**For short entries:**
- Identify a bearish candle at or near a key level (resistance, VWAP from above)
- The trigger price is the LOW of that candle
- Enter when the next candle breaks below the trigger candle's low
- Stop is the HIGH of the trigger candle

**Why this works:** The trigger candle gives you a defined risk. You know BEFORE entering exactly how much you can lose per share. This allows precise position sizing (shares = max risk / trigger candle range).

**Optimal trigger candle characteristics:**
- Tight range (small candle body) = small risk per share = larger position or better R-multiple
- Strong close (closes near the high for longs, near the low for shorts) = shows conviction
- At or near a key level = adds confluence
- Volume at or below average = calm before the storm

## 7.6 Scaling In

Scaling in means entering a position in multiple tranches rather than all at once.

**When scaling in works:**
- You have high conviction but the timing is uncertain
- The stock is at a key level and you want confirmation before going full size
- Entry example: 1/3 at trigger candle break, 1/3 at first pullback hold, 1/3 at momentum confirmation

**When scaling in is just averaging down (DON'T DO THIS):**
- You entered a trade, it went against you, and you're adding more to "improve your average"
- This is how small losses become account-destroying losses
- If the trade goes against you, cut it. Don't add to it

**The difference:** Scaling in is PLANNED before the trade. "I will enter 200 shares at $10.50, add 200 at $10.40 if VWAP holds, add 200 at $10.55 if it breaks the ORB high." Averaging down is REACTIVE — "I bought at $10.50, it's at $10.20, let me buy more to improve my cost basis." The first is a strategy. The second is denial.

## 7.7 Missed Entries and FOMO

You identified the setup, defined the entry, and... the stock ran without you. It broke above the ORB high while you were looking at another chart. It's now up 3% from where your entry would have been.

**What to do: Nothing.**

The trade is over. You missed it. The next setup is already forming somewhere else. Entering a trade 3% above your planned entry means:
- Your stop is now 3% further from your target (worse risk/reward)
- You're buying at a higher price than planned (higher risk)
- You're making an emotional decision, not a planned one
- You're likely chasing, which has the worst win rate of any entry type

**The FOMO spiral:** Miss a trade → chase the next one → enter with bad risk/reward → it reverses → take a loss → feel frustrated → size up on the next trade to "make back the loss" → take a bigger loss. This spiral destroys accounts in hours.

**The antidote:** Accept that you will miss good trades. You will miss 20-40% of the setups you identify. That's fine. You don't need to catch them all. You need to catch 3-5 good ones per day and execute them well. Missing a trade costs you $0. Chasing a trade costs you real money.

**Rule:** If the stock is more than 1R (one risk unit) past your planned entry, it's not your trade anymore. Let it go.
